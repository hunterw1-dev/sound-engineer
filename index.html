By Danny Ross

From Taylor Swift To Lorde, This Woman Is Sculpting The Sound Of Pop

Danny Ross · Former Contributor · May 17, 2018

Music Engineer Laura Sisk

You never really know how your favorite song was recorded. Maybe you've envisioned Jay-Z in a vocal booth rapping the verses of "99 Problems," or The Beach Boys crowded around a microphone crooning "God Only Knows." Laura Sisk doesn't have to imagine. Day in and day out, she's in the studio alongside super-producer Jack Antonoff recording their friends — who just happen to be the biggest pop stars in the world.

In the last year alone, she engineered milestone records for Taylor Swift, Lorde, St. Vincent, P!nk, Sia, ZAYN and Bleachers. She also won a Grammy for her work on Swift's 1989.

"It's just hectic and exciting, and I wouldn't want it any other way," Sisk says of working with different artists. She describes each artist as having a different sense of artistry and says it is cool to witness masters of their craft at work.

While recording Lorde's Melodrama, Sisk focused on keeping the integrity of the singer's hauntingly beautiful voice front and center. She recalls editing "Liability Reprise" and experiencing an existential crisis as the repeated lyric built in intensity.

For Taylor Swift's Reputation, Sisk describes the live strings and horns recorded for "Look What You Made Me Do" as part of a bold sonic departure. Taylor and Jack often begin with a conversation, then create a song that embodies that conversation. Sisk's role is to let Taylor's voice shine while capturing her harmonies and ad libs.

In the studio, it is frequently just Sisk, Jack, and an artist in a room. Sisk edits while Jack chops vocals or plays instruments. Jack prepares every instrument and microphone before the artist arrives so the creative process is never interrupted. They may use two rooms, depending on what is comfortable for the artist and what the session requires.

Sisk explains that Jack often starts with an AKAI MPC drum machine, while organic and electronic sounds are blended through live performances, analog synthesizers, and creative editing. They avoid making demos when they are in an incredible studio, and some first-day recordings become part of the final mix.

To achieve a great bass tone, Sisk listens on many systems, including car speakers and inexpensive earbuds. She emphasizes knowing your speakers and remembering that most listeners hear music through cheap laptop speakers or streamed MP3s.

Sisk grew up in Marin County, north of San Francisco. She played piano, loved The Beatles, and played oboe in high school. When her band teacher became ill and the school needed recordings, she volunteered to figure it out — and discovered that she loved recording.

She studied in the recording program at Indiana University, where experimental music expanded her interests. After school, she worked with producer John Hill, first as an assistant and then as a full-time engineer. Her projects included records by Phantogram, Portugal. The Man, and Wavves.

Sisk met Jack Antonoff through the first Bleachers record, Strange Desire. When she went freelance, Jack needed an engineer for Taylor Swift's 1989; their first songs together included "Out Of The Woods," "I Wish You Would," and "You Are In Love."

She explains that the word "producer" covers many roles, from reshaping songs and melodies to making beats or refining technical details. Sisk also does vocal production: recording artists, helping them find their tone and harmonies, then editing, tuning, and comping their takes.

To make singers comfortable, Sisk learns each artist's language and preferences. Some artists want reverb while others prefer a dry vocal. She and Jack often set up vocals in the control room so the artist can sit beside them rather than communicate through glass.

Sisk describes working with St. Vincent's inventive guitar tones and Tune-Yards' live Ableton performances as examples of the experimental sounds that can emerge in a session. Every record, she says, becomes experimental in some way.

On the lack of women in technical music jobs, Sisk says the industry remains skewed but points to more young women entering STEM and music-business fields. She highlights organizations such as Women's Audio Mission that support young women making records.

Her advice to up-and-coming engineers and musicians is simple: don't be afraid of mistakes. She has made many, including recording at the wrong sample rate, but learned that almost no mistake is permanent. "If I had just hit the button, and it had worked, I wouldn't know what I know."
